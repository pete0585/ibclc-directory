# Lactation Consultant: How to Describe Your Goals at the First Meeting

A first meeting with a lactation consultant can feel like a lot of ground to cover in a short time. You may be tired, emotional, or unsure what to say first. One of the most useful things you can do is arrive with a clear sense of what you want to work toward, and a simple way to describe it.

This guide walks through how to describe your goals at a first meeting with a lactation consultant. It is a decision guide for provider selection and preparation, not medical advice. It does not diagnose any condition, promise any outcome, or replace guidance from a qualified professional. If you have concerns about your health or your baby's health, contact a qualified healthcare provider.

## Why describing goals matters

A lactation consultant's role is to help you work toward feeding goals that fit your situation. When your goals are vague, the visit can drift toward general conversation. When your goals are specific, the consultant can focus the appointment, ask sharper questions, and help you plan next steps that match what you actually want.

Describing goals is not the same as making demands. It is a way to give the consultant a clear target so the two of you can decide together what is realistic and what support might look like. The consultant may also help you notice goals you had not named, such as comfort, rest, or confidence.

## The three layers of a feeding goal

Most feeding goals sit in one of three layers. Naming the layer helps you describe the goal without over-explaining.

1. **The outcome layer.** What you want to be true in the near future. For example, a feeding routine that feels manageable, or a plan you can follow when you are alone.
2. **The experience layer.** How you want feeding to feel. For example, less pain, less worry, or more confidence.
3. **The logistics layer.** The practical shape of support. For example, how often you can attend appointments, whether you prefer in-person or remote visits, or how follow-up would work.

When you describe a goal, try to include at least one layer. If you can include two, the consultant has more to work with.

## A checklist for describing your goals

Use this checklist before the meeting. You do not need to answer every item perfectly. The goal is to arrive with a few clear sentences.

- **Name the main goal in one sentence.** Keep it plain. Example: "I want feeding to feel less stressful within the next few weeks."
- **Name what has been hardest.** Example: "The hardest part is not knowing whether a feeding went well."
- **Name what you have already tried.** Example: "I have tried changing positions and timing."
- **Name what you want from this visit.** Example: "I want a plan I can follow between now and our next check-in."
- **Name your constraints.** Example: "I can only attend appointments on weekday mornings."
- **Name your preferences.** Example: "I would rather start with an in-person visit if that is possible."
- **Name your questions.** Example: "I want to understand what follow-up usually looks like."
- **Name what would count as progress.** Example: "If I feel more confident after two weeks, that would be progress for me."

You can write these on a note and bring it. Many consultants welcome a written list because it keeps the visit focused.

## How to phrase goals without over-committing

Some people worry that describing a goal will lock them into something they cannot sustain. You can describe a goal as a direction rather than a promise.

- Instead of "I will feed only in one way," try "My goal right now is to work toward a routine that fits our family."
- Instead of "I need this fixed today," try "I would like to understand what a realistic next step looks like."
- Instead of "I have failed at this," try "What I have tried so far has not felt workable, and I want help thinking through options."

This kind of phrasing keeps the conversation open. It also gives the consultant room to explain what they can and cannot help with.

## Hypothetical examples (clearly labeled as examples)

These examples are hypothetical and are meant to show how goal descriptions can sound. They are not case studies, and they do not describe real people or real outcomes.

**Example A: A parent who wants less pain.**

> "My main goal is for feeding to feel less painful. The hardest part is that I dread the next feeding. I have tried changing position. I want to leave today with two or three things to try, and I want to know what signs would mean I should check in with a healthcare provider."

This example names the outcome, the experience, and a request for a plan. It also leaves room for the consultant to involve other professionals if needed.

**Example B: A parent who wants a workable routine.**

> "My goal is a feeding routine that works when I am alone during the day. I am not sure what that looks like yet. I want to talk through timing and what to do when a feeding does not go as planned. I can attend appointments on weekday mornings."

This example names a constraint and a preference, which helps with logistics.

**Example C: A parent who wants confidence.**

> "My goal is to feel more confident about what I am seeing. I do not need everything solved today. I want to understand what to watch for and when to reach out for more help."

This example sets a realistic expectation and asks for guidance on next steps.

**Example D: A parent comparing providers.**

> "I am still deciding between a few providers. My goal today is to understand how you usually structure a first visit and what follow-up tends to look like. I want to know whether my goals fit the way you work."

This example is useful when you are selecting a provider rather than already committed to one.

## Questions to ask when describing goals

As you describe your goals, you may want to ask questions that help you compare providers and understand the plan.

- How do you usually decide what to focus on in a first visit?
- What does follow-up typically look like, and how would we decide together?
- How do you handle goals that change over time?
- What would you want me to bring or prepare for a next visit?
- When would you suggest involving another professional?

These questions are about process, not about clinical promises. A provider should be able to explain their approach without guaranteeing a specific result.

## What to do if you feel unsure

It is normal to feel unsure about your goals. You can say that directly: "I am not sure what my goal should be yet. I know I want feeding to feel more manageable, and I would like help naming the rest."

A consultant can help you sort goals into what to work on first and what can wait. You do not need a perfect script. You need enough clarity to start a useful conversation.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Trying to cover everything at once.** Pick one or two main goals for the first meeting.
- **Using vague words only.** "Better" and "easier" are fine starting points, but add one concrete detail.
- **Assuming the consultant knows your context.** Briefly name your constraints and preferences.
- **Treating the first meeting as a final answer.** Goals can change, and that is expected.
- **Confusing goals with guarantees.** A goal is a direction; it is not a promise of a specific outcome.

## A short pre-meeting script

If you want a simple structure, try this:

> "My main goal is ____. The hardest part has been ____. I have tried ____. What I want from this visit is ____. A constraint I have is ____. A question I have is ____."

You can fill in the blanks in a few minutes. Bring the note with you.

## When to consult a qualified professional

This article is general information about preparing for a conversation. It is not medical, legal, or individualized treatment advice. If you have concerns about your health or your baby's health, or if you are unsure whether a symptom needs attention, contact a qualified healthcare professional. A lactation consultant can be part of your support team, but they do not replace your other care providers.

## Key takeaways

- Describe goals in one or two clear sentences.
- Name the outcome, the experience, and the logistics when you can.
- Bring a short note with your goals, constraints, and questions.
- Treat goals as directions, not guarantees.
- Ask process questions to compare providers.
- Involve qualified professionals for health concerns.

A first meeting is a conversation, not a test. The clearer you can be about what you want, the easier it is for a lactation consultant to help you decide what to do next.
