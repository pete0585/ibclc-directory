# Lactation Consultant: Questions About Privacy and Information Sharing

When you contact a lactation consultant, you are usually sharing details that feel personal. You may describe how feeding has been going, what your body has been doing, how your baby has been behaving, what other clinicians have said, and how you are feeling emotionally. That information can be deeply identifying. It is reasonable to want to know how it will be handled before you say more than you are comfortable saying.

This guide is about privacy and information sharing as a provider-selection question. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for guidance from a qualified professional who knows your situation. Privacy rules can differ by location, by the type of provider, and by the setting where care happens. The goal here is to help you ask better questions, notice what you are being told, and make a decision that fits your comfort level.

## Why privacy deserves a place on your provider checklist

Many families choose a lactation consultant by looking at location, availability, appointment format, and whether the provider seems approachable. Those are useful filters. Privacy is another one, and it interacts with all of them.

A provider who works in a hospital, a community clinic, a private practice, or entirely remotely may have different systems for storing notes, communicating with you, and coordinating with other clinicians. The same provider may also offer more than one type of appointment, and the privacy practices may differ between them.

You do not need to become an expert in health privacy law to have a useful conversation. You mainly need to know what you are being asked to share, who may see it, how it is stored, and what choices you have. Those questions are reasonable to ask before the first appointment, not only after.

## Start with what you are actually sharing

Before you think about policies, it helps to notice the categories of information that a lactation consultation can involve. Some examples:

- **Feeding details.** How often feeding happens, how long it lasts, what has been tried, what seems to help, and what seems to make things harder.
- **Health history.** Relevant medical background for you and your baby, medications, allergies, past procedures, and prior feeding experiences.
- **Observations.** What a provider may see or hear during an in-person or video appointment, including positioning, latch, and how you and your baby respond.
- **Emotional context.** Stress, exhaustion, anxiety, mood changes, and the practical pressures of recovery and newborn care.
- **Contact and logistics.** Your name, address, phone number, email, appointment times, and payment or insurance details.

Not every consultation will touch every category. But it can be useful to picture the full range so you can decide what you are willing to share at each stage.

## A checklist of privacy questions to ask

You can use this as a script. You do not have to ask everything at once, and you do not have to ask in any particular order. The point is to get clear answers.

### Before the first appointment

1. **What information do you need from me before we meet?** This tells you what the provider considers necessary versus optional.
2. **How should I send it?** Ask specifically about email, text, online forms, patient portals, phone calls, and paper records. Some methods are more secure than others.
3. **Who will see what I send?** Ask whether anyone besides the provider reviews intake forms, messages, or notes.
4. **Do you have a written privacy notice or consent form?** If so, ask to see it before the appointment.
5. **What happens if I do not want to share something?** A good answer will explain whether the provider can still work with you and what the limits might be.

### During and after the appointment

6. **How are notes stored?** Ask whether notes are paper, electronic, or both, and who can access them.
7. **Will you contact my other providers?** If so, ask which ones, for what purpose, and whether you will be asked first.
8. **How do you communicate between appointments?** Ask about email, text, portal messages, and phone calls, and whether any of those are encrypted or otherwise protected.
9. **How long do you keep records?** You may not get an exact number, but the answer can tell you how the practice thinks about retention.
10. **What if I want a copy of my records or want to correct something?** Ask how that request works.
11. **What happens if I want to stop working with you?** Ask how your information is handled after the relationship ends.

### If the appointment is remote

12. **What platform do you use?** Ask whether it is a dedicated health platform or a general video tool.
13. **Who else could be in the room or on the call?** This matters if a student, assistant, or colleague may be present.
14. **What should I do if I am not in a private space?** Ask whether you can reschedule, use audio only, or adjust what you share.

## Concrete hypothetical examples

These examples are invented to illustrate how privacy questions can play out. They are not real cases, and they are not recommendations for any particular provider.

**Example 1: The intake form asks for more than expected.**
A parent fills out a new-client form that asks for their baby's full name, birth date, insurance number, home address, and a detailed medical history. The form also asks whether the parent has experienced anxiety or depression. The parent is comfortable sharing feeding information but not the mental health question at this stage. They ask the provider whether the mental health question is required. The provider explains that it helps with planning but is optional, and that the parent can leave it blank and discuss it later if they choose. The parent decides to leave it blank for now and to revisit it at the appointment.

**Example 2: A provider wants to coordinate with a pediatric office.**
A lactation consultant suggests sending a summary of the visit to the baby's pediatric clinician. The parent wants the pediatric office to have the feeding information but does not want the emotional context included. The provider and parent agree on what will be shared and what will be left out. The parent asks to see the summary before it is sent. This kind of agreement is worth asking about in advance.

**Example 3: A remote appointment in a shared home.**
A parent books a video consultation but lives in a small apartment with visiting relatives. The parent is worried about being overheard. They ask the provider whether they can use headphones, keep the camera off, or reschedule. The provider offers to start with a phone call and to move to video only if the parent feels comfortable. The parent chooses the phone call.

**Example 4: A follow-up message arrives by text.**
After an appointment, a provider sends a follow-up text asking how feeding has been going. The parent had assumed all communication would happen through a patient portal. They ask the provider to use the portal instead. The provider agrees and notes the preference. This is a small example of how privacy preferences can be adjusted after care begins.

## What to listen for in the answers

You are not looking for a perfect answer. You are looking for signs that the provider takes the question seriously and can explain their process in plain language.

Helpful signals can include:

- Clear descriptions of how information is stored and who can access it.
- Willingness to put privacy preferences in writing.
- Acknowledgment that some questions depend on the setting, the location, or the type of provider.
- An invitation to ask again later if your comfort level changes.

Less helpful signals can include:

- Vague answers that do not address where information goes.
- Pressure to share more than you want to share before you have decided.
- A refusal to explain who else may see your information.
- Claims that privacy is "not an issue" without any explanation of why.

## Questions about sharing with family and support people

Privacy is not only about institutions. It is also about the people around you. You may want a partner, a parent, a friend, or a doula involved in appointments. You may also want some conversations to stay between you and the provider.

It can help to decide in advance:

- Who, if anyone, you want in the room or on the call.
- Whether that person can ask questions directly or should defer to you.
- What you are comfortable having repeated to others afterward.
- Whether you want the provider to speak with a support person separately.

You can tell the provider these preferences at the start. A provider who respects them will usually confirm them back to you.

## When to seek additional guidance

Privacy rules and obligations can vary. If you have specific concerns about your rights, about a provider's obligations, or about a situation that feels wrong, consider consulting a qualified professional who can advise you based on your location and circumstances. That might be a patient advocate, an attorney, or another trusted advisor. This article cannot resolve those questions for you.

## A short pre-appointment checklist

- Decide what you are willing to share before the first contact.
- Ask how information is sent, stored, and accessed.
- Ask who else may see your information.
- Ask what happens if you decline to share something.
- Ask how communication will work between appointments.
- Ask how to get a copy of your records.
- Confirm your preferences in writing if possible.
- Revisit the conversation if your comfort level changes.
