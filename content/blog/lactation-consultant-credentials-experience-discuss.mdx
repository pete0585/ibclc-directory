## Start with the conversation, not the label

Choosing a lactation consultant often happens during a stretch of life that is already full — recovering, feeding, sleeping in fragments, fielding advice from every direction. In that context, it is tempting to pick the first available name and hope for the best. A more durable approach is to treat provider selection as a short, structured conversation. You are not evaluating someone's worth as a practitioner. You are gathering the specific information you need to decide whether this person is a good fit for your situation.

This guide focuses on two threads that come up again and again in that conversation: **credentials** and **experience**. It offers a checklist you can adapt, plus hypothetical examples that are clearly labeled as examples. Nothing here is clinical advice, and nothing here replaces a conversation with a qualified professional.

## Why credentials and experience are separate questions

It is easy to blend these into one question — "are you qualified?" — but they answer different things.

- **Credentials** describe formal training, certification, and the scope a practitioner is authorized to work within.
- **Experience** describes the range of situations a practitioner has actually worked with, and how they approach the parts of feeding support that are not covered by a certificate.

A provider can be strongly credentialed and still be a poor fit for your particular needs. A provider can have deep hands-on experience and still hold credentials that matter to you for other reasons. Asking about both gives you a fuller picture.

## A checklist for the credentials conversation

Use this as a starting point. You do not need to ask every question in one sitting, and you can ask some of them by email or through a contact form before you ever book.

### 1. What is your current certification or license, and who issues it?

Ask for the specific name of the credential and the issuing body. If you want to verify it, ask how you can do so. Credentialing bodies typically maintain public directories, and a practitioner who is proud of their credential will usually be glad to point you to the right place.

### 2. What is the scope of your practice?

Scope describes what a practitioner is trained and authorized to do, and — just as importantly — what falls outside it. Ask directly: "What kinds of situations do you handle yourself, and when would you refer someone to another professional?" A clear, specific answer is usually a good sign.

### 3. How do you keep your credential current?

Many credentials require continuing education or periodic renewal. Ask how the practitioner stays current, and whether there are areas they have focused on recently.

### 4. Are you covered by any professional insurance or affiliated with a professional body?

This is a practical question, not a judgment. If the answer is unclear, ask a follow-up.

### 5. Do you work with any particular populations or settings?

Some practitioners focus on hospital settings, some on home visits, some on specific feeding situations. Knowing where someone spends most of their time helps you predict how relevant their day-to-day experience will be to yours.

> **Example (hypothetical, not a real provider):** A parent asks a consultant, "Do you mostly see families in the first two weeks, or do you also work with older babies?" The consultant explains that most of their visits happen in the first month, but they also take referrals for later-stage feeding questions. That answer tells the parent something concrete about the practitioner's typical caseload.

## A checklist for the experience conversation

Credentials tell you what someone is trained to do. Experience tells you what they have actually done, and how they think about it.

### 1. How long have you been practicing, and in what settings?

Years matter less than settings. A practitioner who has worked in a hospital, a clinic, and in home visits has seen feeding support from several angles.

### 2. What kinds of situations do you see most often?

This is not a test. It is a way to gauge whether the practitioner's typical caseload overlaps with what you are looking for.

### 3. How do you approach a first visit?

Ask what a typical first consultation looks like — how long it lasts, what happens during it, and what the practitioner hopes to learn. This helps you picture the visit before you commit.

### 4. How do you handle situations outside your experience?

No practitioner has seen everything. The useful question is what happens when something is unfamiliar: do they consult colleagues, refer out, or research further?

### 5. What does follow-up look like?

Ask whether follow-up is included, how it is scheduled, and how you would reach the practitioner between visits if something changes.

> **Example (hypothetical, not a real provider):** A parent asks, "If we meet and you think I need to see someone with a different specialty, how would that work?" The consultant describes a referral process and names the kinds of professionals they typically refer to. The parent now knows the practitioner is comfortable with the boundaries of their own scope.

## Putting the two threads together

Once you have answers to the credential questions and the experience questions, you can compare providers on a few practical dimensions:

- **Fit with your situation.** Does the practitioner's typical caseload overlap with what you are navigating?
- **Clarity of scope.** Do they describe what they do and do not handle in specific terms?
- **Communication style.** Did the conversation feel rushed, or did you have room to ask follow-ups?
- **Logistics.** Do their availability, location, or visit format work for your life right now?
- **Follow-up plan.** Do you understand what happens after the first visit?

## What not to expect from this conversation

A few things are worth naming, because they come up in almost every provider-selection conversation and they can quietly distort it.

- **No one can promise an outcome.** A practitioner who guarantees a specific result is telling you something about their communication style, not about what will happen.
- **Credentials are not a ranking.** A longer list of letters after a name does not automatically mean a better fit for you.
- **Experience is not a substitute for scope.** Someone who has seen a lot of one kind of situation is not automatically equipped for a different one.
- **This is not individualized advice.** Everything in this guide is general. Your own situation deserves a conversation with a qualified professional.

## A short pre-booking script you can adapt

If you would rather not improvise, here is a compact version of the checklist you can send by email or read aloud on a call:

1. What is your current certification or license, and who issues it?
2. What is the scope of your practice — what do you handle, and what do you refer out?
3. How long have you been practicing, and in what settings?
4. What does a first visit typically look like?
5. What does follow-up look like?

You do not need perfect answers to every question. You are looking for a practitioner whose answers are specific, honest about limits, and easy to understand.

## Where to go from here

Provider selection is not a single decision — it is a short series of small, specific questions. Credentials tell you what someone is trained and authorized to do. Experience tells you what they have actually worked with. Follow-up tells you what happens after the first visit. Ask about all three, in whatever order makes sense for you, and let the answers guide the choice.
